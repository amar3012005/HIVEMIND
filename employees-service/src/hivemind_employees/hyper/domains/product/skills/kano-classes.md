when: a feature list mixes basics, improvements, and delighters as if they were equivalent, or the plan invests in novelty while known basics are still broken
---

KANO CLASSIFICATION method (Noriaki Kano et al., 1984, "Attractive Quality and Must-Be Quality"):
1. CLASSIFY every candidate feature into one of five satisfaction classes:
   - MUST-BE — assumed; presence buys no satisfaction, absence causes severe dissatisfaction (login works, data is not lost).
   - PERFORMANCE (one-dimensional) — linear; more is better, and customers will pay proportionally (speed, capacity, coverage).
   - ATTRACTIVE (delighter) — unexpected; presence delights, absence costs nothing because it was not expected.
   - INDIFFERENT — no effect either way. Cut it.
   - REVERSE — presence actively dissatisfies this segment (added steps, forced onboarding, features that read as bloat).
2. ASK, DON'T ASSUME — classify from the paired Kano question per feature: the FUNCTIONAL form ("how do you feel if it has this?") and the DYSFUNCTIONAL form ("how do you feel if it does not?"), each answered on the like/expect/neutral/tolerate/dislike scale. The pair maps to a class on the standard evaluation table. Segments differ: a delighter for one is indifferent for another, so classify per segment, never for "users" in general.
3. SEQUENCE — every MUST-BE gap outranks every delighter; no amount of delight compensates for a missing basic. Then invest in the PERFORMANCE attributes this segment actually pays for. Fund at most one ATTRACTIVE bet per cycle.
4. RE-CLASSIFY ON A CADENCE — classes decay. Today's delighter becomes tomorrow's must-be as competitors adopt it. Date every classification and re-run it.
5. Mark any class assigned without customer input as UNVERIFIED and name the question that would settle it.
